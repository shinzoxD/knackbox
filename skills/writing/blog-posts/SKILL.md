# Blog Posts

Write posts a busy person finishes. Every section must earn the scroll:
concrete claims, real examples, no throat-clearing.

## Before drafting

Establish (from the request, or by asking at most one question):
audience, goal (teach / persuade / announce), target length, and voice.
If the user has prior posts or a style note, match it. Otherwise default
to: first person, plain words, short sentences, contractions.

Capture a one-line **promise**: what the reader can do or know by the end.
If you cannot state the promise, the topic is too vague — sharpen it first.

## Structure

1. **Hook (first 2–3 sentences)**: a specific problem, surprising fact, or
   scene — never "In today's fast-paced world" or a dictionary definition.
2. **Promise**: one sentence on what the reader gets by the end.
3. **Body**: 3–5 sections with descriptive subheads (a skimmer reading only
   subheads should get the argument). One idea per section, each grounded
   by an example, number, or mini-story.
4. **Close**: restate the takeaway in fresh words + one clear next step.
   No "In conclusion".

Optional mid-post pattern for how-to pieces:

```markdown
## The mistake
## A better default
## How to do it (steps)
## What good looks like
```

## Rules

1. Specific beats general: "cut build time from 9 min to 40 s" not
   "significantly improved performance". Invent nothing — mark
   placeholders like [YOUR METRIC] when the user must fill facts in.
2. Cut filler on sight: "very", "really", "it's important to note",
   "delve", "in order to", "landscape", "robust solution".
3. Vary sentence length; read one paragraph aloud mentally per section.
4. Titles: give 3 options — one direct/SEO, one curiosity, one
   contrarian. Never promise what the post doesn't deliver.
5. Format for the web: paragraphs ≤ 4 lines, bold the 3–5 load-bearing
   phrases, use a list only when order or enumeration matters.
6. Prefer one worked example over three abstract principles.
7. If rewriting a draft, preserve the author's claims and voice; fix
   structure and clarity first, word choice second.

## Good vs bad

**Bad hook:** "Content marketing is more important than ever in 2026."
**Good hook:** "We published 40 posts last year. Three drove 80% of signups.
Here is what those three shared."

**Bad section:** a bullet list of tips with no scene or numbers.
**Good section:** one tip, one short story or metric, one "try this Monday"
sentence.

## Edge cases

- **Topic too broad** ("write about AI"): propose 3 sharper angles and
  draft the one you'd pick, saying why.
- **SEO request**: weave the keyword into title, first 100 words, and one
  subhead — never keyword-stuff. Meta description ≤ 155 characters when asked.
- **Expert audience**: skip 101 definitions; open at the frontier of what
  they already know.
- **Newsletter**: shorter paragraphs, one primary CTA, P.S. optional; still
  needs a hook and a single point.
- **Thin source material**: draft with labeled `[need fact]` gaps rather than
  inventing statistics or quotes.
- **Ghostwriting**: match supplied voice samples; if none, ask for 3 adjectives
  (e.g. direct, warm, skeptical) and stick to them.

## Delivery checklist

Before handing back:

- [ ] Hook works without the title
- [ ] Subheads alone tell the argument
- [ ] No invented metrics or quotes
- [ ] One clear next step at the end
- [ ] Title options included (unless user forbade them)
